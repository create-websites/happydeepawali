National Day of Mourning: Indigenous Perspectives on Thanksgiving

Learn about the National Day of Mourning in Plymouth, its beginnings in 1970, and why numerous Indigenous individuals view Thanksgiving as a time for reflection and advocacy.

Read the guideAll 20 articles
🍁 ThanksgivingHistory, Heritage & Perspectives

A different meaning on the same day

Since 1970, the United American Indians of New England (UAINE) has been hosting a National Day of Mourning in Plymouth, Massachusetts on U.S. Thanksgiving. UAINE views this day as a time to pay tribute to Indigenous ancestors and their strength, reflect on the impacts of colonization, and speak out against persistent racism and oppression.

This observance does not simply mirror the family-centered Thanksgiving celebrated by many Americans; rather, it exists alongside it, showcasing how one national holiday can hold contrasting historical significance.

How the observance began

UAINE links the inaugural National Day of Mourning to Wamsutta Frank James, an Aquinnah Wampanoag leader invited to speak at a 1970 event commemorating the Pilgrims' arrival. However, organizers opposed his speech, which challenged traditional narratives of colonial history.

The protest evolved into a yearly Indigenous-led event held at Cole's Hill in Plymouth.

Why language and attribution matter

When discussing the National Day of Mourning, it is crucial to credit the individuals and groups sharing their perspectives. UAINE clearly presents Thanksgiving as a day symbolizing genocide, land theft, and cultural erasure for numerous Native individuals. This viewpoint serves as the foundation for their commemoration.

Don't lump all Native nations together. Indigenous communities have varying perspectives on Thanksgiving.

What respectful learning can look like

Families and schools can combine gratitude practices with learning about the accurate local history: discover the indigenous peoples whose homelands you reside on, explore literature written by Native authors, back modern Native organizations, and steer clear of stereotypical "Pilgrim and Indian" costumes. The aim is to promote historical understanding, not guilt or romanticizing the past.

Frequently asked questions

When did the National Day of Mourning begin?

UAINE has organized the observance in Plymouth since 1970.

Do all Native Americans view Thanksgiving in the same way?

Indigenous nations and individuals are not homogeneous; the National Day of Mourning is reflective of a specific Indigenous tradition and viewpoint, rather than a universal Native perspective.
