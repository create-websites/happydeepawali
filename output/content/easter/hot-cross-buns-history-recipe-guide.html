Hot Cross Buns: History, Tradition and Baking Guide

Discover the history behind hot cross buns and how to bake a batch with a fluffy texture and aromatic flavor for Good Friday.

Read the guideAll 20 articles
🌼 EasterCulinary & Baking Trends

Why the buns are tied to Holy Week

Hot cross buns are closely linked to Good Friday in Britain and other regions with British culinary influences. The combination of spiced dough, dried fruit, and cross decoration transformed this seasonal bun into a well-known Holy Week treat.

Build flavor before baking

Make a slightly enriched yeast dough with cozy spices like cinnamon and nutmeg. Sweeten with raisins, currants, or chopped dried fruit. Allow the dough to rise completely for fluffy buns instead of heavy ones.

Make the cross and finish

Before baking, you can pipe a basic mixture of flour and water onto the shaped buns. Once baked, apply a light glaze like warmed apricot jam or simple syrup for a glossy finish.

Serve fresh or revive gently

Freshly baked hot cross buns are at their best the day they are made, but they can also be toasted with success the following morning. Enjoy them split and spread with butter, or repurpose older buns for delicious bread pudding or a baked breakfast casserole.

Frequently asked questions

Are hot cross buns only eaten on Good Friday?

Although Good Friday is their primary traditional association, bakeries frequently offer them for sale during Lent and the Easter season.

What makes the cross on top?

Numerous contemporary recipes call for piping a mixture of flour and water before baking, while other customs involve using icing after baking.
