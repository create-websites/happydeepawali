Regional Celebrations: Bhai Phota, Bhau Beej, and Yama Dwitiya

Travel through Bengal, Maharashtra, Gujarat, Nepal and southern Indian traditions to see how one sibling festival takes on many local expressions.

Bhai Dooj Guide4 min readUpdated October 21, 2026
Bhai Dooj  ›  Regional Traditions  ›  Article 3

Ask ten families how they observe Bhai Dooj and you may hear several names, distinct prayers and different menus. In Bengal the day is often called Bhai Phota; in Maharashtra, Bhau Beej; in other regions, Bhai Bij, Bhai Tika or Yama Dwitiya. The variety is part of the festival’s richness.

The shared thread is the bond between siblings, typically expressed through a ceremonial mark, prayers, food and gifts. What changes is the language, sequence and symbolism around those acts.

Bengal: Bhai Phota and the art of a heartfelt blessing

In Bengali households, Bhai Phota is an important family occasion in the Diwali–Kali Puja season. Sisters place a ceremonial mark, or phota, on a brother’s forehead while reciting a blessing. The substances used for the mark: often involving sandalwood paste or other familiar household ingredients: vary by family.

A meal follows in many homes, with Bengali sweets such as sandesh or other family favorites. Some families exchange gifts; others make the meal and conversation the central event. The sound of a familiar prayer, spoken in Bengali, often gives the day its most personal character.

Rather than attempting to reproduce a “correct” Bengali phota from an online checklist, ask an elder which words and ingredients your family uses. That is how living traditions are preserved.

Maharashtra and Goa: Bhau Beej

In Maharashtra and parts of neighboring regions, the day is known as Bhau Beej, literally emphasizing the brother and the lunar day. Sisters may welcome their brothers with aarti and tilak; a festive meal and a gift exchange commonly follow. Menus might include puran poli, basundi, shrikhand or dishes that belong to the household’s own Diwali table.

Some traditions also include a symbolic observance connected with the moon when a brother cannot be present. Not every household follows it, but the practice illustrates how the festival has accommodated absence and longing for generations.

Gujarat and northern India: Bhai Bij and Bhaiya Dooj

In Gujarat, Bhai Bij is a familiar name; in much of North India, people say Bhaiya Dooj or Bhai Dooj. Sisters commonly invite brothers home, apply a tilak, offer sweets and share food. Particular objects placed on the puja thali may reflect community or family practice.

The story of Yama’s visit to Yamuna is especially associated with the name Yama Dwitiya. Some communities connect the day with a visit to a river or a symbolic bath, but no single ritual should be presented as obligatory for every region.

Southern Indian observances: Yama Dwitiya and Bhagini Hasta Bhojanam

Across southern India, observance is more varied and sometimes less prominent than in the north. Names such as Yama Dwitiya, Bhratri Dwitiya and Bhagini Hasta Bhojanam appear in regional calendars and traditional descriptions. Bhagini Hasta Bhojanam highlights the idea of eating food offered by a sister.

It would be misleading to claim that every Tamil, Telugu, Kannada or Malayalam household celebrates an identical Bhai Dooj ceremony. Some families place it within Deepavali customs; others do not mark it separately. If you are documenting regional culture, identify the specific community whose practice you are describing.

Nepal: Bhai Tika during Tihar

In Nepal, Bhai Tika is a major part of Tihar, the festival season related to Diwali. Sisters place a tika on brothers’ foreheads, and celebrations may include a multicolored mark, garlands, food and gifts, depending on family tradition.

Although Bhai Tika is often grouped with Bhai Dooj, it has a distinct local calendar context, songs and ceremonies. That distinctiveness deserves recognition rather than treating it as a duplicate of northern Indian practice.

Plan a regional family celebration without flattening the differences

Choose one family tradition as your anchor. Keep the words of the blessing in the language spoken at home, cook at least one dish associated with your family, and invite elders to explain what each gesture means. If siblings have married into different regions, exchange customs: one year make Bengali sandesh, another year serve Maharashtrian basundi.

A simple family photo album with names for the festival in multiple languages can become a lasting record. The day’s beauty lies in its many expressions: and the love that connects them.

Frequently asked questions

What is Bhai Phota?

Bhai Phota is the Bengali form of the sibling festival, noted for a sister’s ceremonial forehead mark and blessing.

Is Bhai Tika the same as Bhai Dooj?

They are related sibling festivals observed in the Diwali/Tihar season, but Nepal’s Bhai Tika has its own distinctive rituals and cultural setting.
